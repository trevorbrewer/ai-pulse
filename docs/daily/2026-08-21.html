Safety Research

ConceptGuard: Benchmarking Context-Sensitive Unlearning in Large Language ModelsarXiv

Researchers introduced ConceptGuard, a benchmark designed to evaluate machine unlearning in LLMs under context-sensitive conditions that existing benchmarks ignore. Current unlearning evaluations rely on disjoint forget and retain sets of independent facts and measure success through simple factual recall, a framing that fails to capture how knowledge is interconnected in real usage. ConceptGuard tests whether a model that forgets a target fact also appropriately handles contextually related information, raising the bar for what constitutes successful unlearning. The work highlights a gap between reported benchmark performance and the practical safety guarantees that selective knowledge removal is meant to provide.

AI4AI-Bench: Benchmarking LLM Agents in Algorithmic Design for Recursive Self-ImprovementarXiv

AI4AI-Bench is a new benchmark that evaluates whether LLM agents can propose improvements to the training algorithms used to produce AI systems, a process the authors frame as recursive self-improvement (RSI). The benchmark focuses on the training algorithm itself — objective functions and update rules — because a better algorithm improves the compute-to-capability ratio for every subsequent training run. By measuring agent performance on algorithmic design tasks, the work provides an empirical handle on whether current LLMs can meaningfully participate in their own improvement cycle. The benchmark is notable because it operationalizes a concept that has largely remained theoretical in AI safety discussions.

Efficiency and Routing in AI Systems

Pandora's AI Model Routing Box: Efficient Allocation with Costly Value EstimationarXiv

This paper formalizes query routing across heterogeneous AI systems — multiple models, architectures, or inference-time settings — as an optimization problem where value estimation itself carries a cost. Cheap estimators such as embedding-based predictors are fast but noisy, while expensive evaluators are accurate but slow, creating a trade-off the authors model using a Pandora's box framework from economics. The work derives policies that decide when to pay for better estimates before committing a query to a specialist, balancing quality and efficiency. The approach is relevant to production systems where running every model on every query is cost-prohibitive.

Research Papers: Theory and Mathematics

Information on trajectories: martingales and random timesarXiv

This theoretical paper develops exact variational identities for nonnegative martingales by accounting for information flow along the path space, including at arbitrary random stopping times. The framework recovers classical concentration inequalities — ranging from Ville's inequality to PAC-Bayes bounds — as special cases and quantifies what each one discards relative to the exact identity. A key insight is that the tail controlled by any such bound is itself a relative entropy, which the authors decompose via the chain rule into per-step contributions. The work provides a unified information-theoretic lens for understanding a family of bounds widely used in machine learning theory.

Physical-Support Confidence Sets for Highly Coherent DictionariesarXiv

This paper addresses a calibration problem in sparse signal recovery: when a learned dictionary is highly coherent, the atom support selected by sparse pursuit may not have a unique physical interpretation, because alternative calibration-compatible dictionaries can assign different physical meanings to the same mathematical support. The authors develop resolution-aware physical-support inference that jointly accounts for dictionary uncertainty and measurement noise. The method produces confidence sets over physically meaningful supports rather than point estimates, providing uncertainty quantification aligned with the actual ambiguity in the calibration data. This is particularly relevant for applications such as sensor array processing where physical interpretability matters.

$TCP_α$: Margin-Controlled Confidence estimation for reliable Music Information RetrievalarXiv

Researchers propose TCP-alpha, a post-hoc confidence estimation method that trains a lightweight auxiliary head over a frozen classifier to produce more reliable confidence scores for music information retrieval tasks. The method addresses the overconfidence problem common in deep neural networks, where high confidence scores are assigned even to incorrect predictions. Existing post-hoc targets suffer from label ambiguity that causes overlapping confidence assignments; TCP-alpha introduces margin control to sharpen the separation between confident correct and uncertain or incorrect predictions. The approach is evaluated in the specific domain of music information retrieval, where label ambiguity is particularly prevalent due to subjective genre and mood categories.

Phantom Gains: Auditing Self-Improvement Against a Measured NullarXiv

Researchers have published a study questioning how language model self-improvement is measured, arguing that tracking which individual problems a model gains or loses between evaluations introduces vulnerability to measurement artifacts. The core issue is that differencing two noisy estimates of per-problem accuracy can produce spurious signals of improvement. To test this, the authors ran three rounds of rank-32 LoRA self-training on Qwen3-8B and compared results against a frozen control model pushed through the identical pipeline, providing a concrete null baseline against which claimed gains can be audited. The work raises methodological concerns relevant to any research that judges self-training progress by problem-level transitions rather than aggregate accuracy.
